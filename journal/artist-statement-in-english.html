Writing your artist statement in English

A working method for artists whose first language isn't English. The goal is not perfect English. It is a statement a curator can read in one minute and remember.

The editors · 8 min read

Start in your own language

Write the first draft in the language you think in. Translating a finished thought is easier than thinking in a second language and translating at the same time. Write freely, then underline the three sentences that say something only you could say. Those three sentences are your statement. The rest is notes.

Say what the work is before what it means

Many statements open with ideas: memory, time, the self, the boundary between nature and culture. A reader who has not seen the work cannot hold on to these. Open with what is physically there: the material, the scale, the process, how long a piece takes. Meaning lands better once the reader can picture the object.

Weak: "My work explores the fragility of memory." Stronger: "I paint the same window in my studio every morning, on paper I later soak and tear."

Keep it to about 150 words

Most open calls and gallery submissions ask for a short statement, and readers skim long ones. Aim for three short paragraphs: what you make and how; what you keep returning to and why; what you are working on now. Keep a longer version of 300 to 500 words for catalogues and your website.

Use the first person and the present tense

"I cast", "I collect", "I am working on". This is shorter, clearer and more direct than the passive voice ("works are created through") and it sounds like a person rather than an institution.

Cut the words that appear in every statement

Some words are so common in artist statements that readers stop seeing them. Replace each with something concrete about your own practice:

  • "explore", "investigate", "question": say what you actually do with your hands or your camera.
  • "liminal", "dialogue", "discourse": say between what, with whom, about what.
  • "unique", "innovative": let the description show it.

Translate culture, not just words

If your work depends on a material, place, ritual or term that is well known in Korea or elsewhere in Asia, give the reader one plain line of context the first time it appears. Keep the original word where it matters, in italics, followed by a short explanation. Do not assume the reader knows it, and do not over-explain it either.

Check it like an editor

  1. Read it aloud. Any sentence you run out of breath on is too long.
  2. Ask someone who has never seen your work to describe it back to you from the statement alone.
  3. Run a grammar checker for spelling and articles, but do not accept rewrites that change your meaning or make you sound like someone else.
  4. If you use machine translation, translate the English back into your own language and see whether it still says what you meant.

When to get help

A statement written in plain, slightly imperfect English is better than a polished one that does not sound like you. If you are preparing for an international exhibition, a residency or a gallery approach, it is worth having a native English editor who knows contemporary art read it once. That is the kind of work our English Profile package covers, but this guide is enough to write a strong first version on your own.
